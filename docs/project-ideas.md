# Build a Meeting Layer That Taps Your Shoulder

**Our pick is "Tap", an on-device accessibility layer for video meetings.** The laptop's webcam and microphone listen for the moments a deaf or hard-of-hearing participant usually misses: their name being said, a direct question, "any objections?". When one happens, Tap does three things: a "knock" haptic on the MX Master 4, a visual flash, and a 10-second recap. It also lets non-speaking participants act through face gestures, for example raising their eyebrows to raise a hand or nodding to answer "yes". Tap covers four tracks with a feature for each that you can show live. For Logitech, the webcam becomes the AI's eyes and ears and the mouse becomes its silent voice. For IMC Tech for Good, it is inclusion grounded in documented pain points. For AXA, the model runs on landmarks locally and an LLM is called only when an event fires. For Nexthink, it is a meeting companion that also runs an "is my camera and mic actually OK?" check before every call. The notes found no product or study that offers speech-triggered "you were just addressed" alerts for deaf or hard-of-hearing users, so the core idea is fresh. Every part of the stack can be built in under three hours. The runner-up is a webcam coach that teaches hearing people their first 20 signs in Swiss French Sign Language (LSF-CH), including facial grammar. It is the only concept that seriously reaches Scholé AI, but it carries more risk with the Deaf community and with recognition accuracy. Below are four ranked concepts, each with its tracks, its wow moment, a build plan keyed to the Sat 12:00 to Sun 12:00 schedule, and the main risks. One recommendation follows at the end.

## LauzHack juries reward webcam accessibility, if it is focused and grounded

Past results show this theme wins at LauzHack. In **2025 alone, webcam, gesture or accessibility projects won the Organizers' prize (Birdy, posture and gestures by webcam), Logitech 1st (MX Sense, haptics that guide visually impaired users), Logitech 3rd (JARVIS, a hands-free assistant on webcam and speech) and JetBrains 1st (gesture and eye tracking in the IDE)** ([LauzHack 2025 projects](https://2025.lauzhack.com/projects)). In 2024 the 1st-overall prize went to Converge, an AI agent that prepares meetings ([LauzHack 2024 projects](https://2024.lauzhack.com/projects)). Logitech judges like projects that make Logitech hardware do something new. MX Sense won with the mouse's haptics alone and pitched a market segment that "we can sell mice to", and the 2025 overall winner was itself a Logitech-ecosystem hack that took three prizes ([LauzHack 2025 projects](https://2025.lauzhack.com/projects)).

The challenge wording echoes Logitech's corporate line. CEO Hanneke Faber calls the company's hardware "**the eyes, the ears and the hands of AI**" and a "sensory layer for AI" ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/pro/our-work-is-never-done-logitech-ceo-on-the-trends-shaping-the-future-of-work-and-how-hardware-can-be-the-eyes-the-ears-and-the-hands-of-ai)). She also wants an AI agent in every board meeting ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/109906-logitech-ceo-hanneke-faber-wants-ai-agent-every.html)) and dismisses standalone AI gadgets as "a solution looking for a problem" ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2025/12/04/logitech_chief_ai/)). Logitech's hackaTUM 2025 brief names the pain points: cognitive overload, tab overload and context switching ([hackaTUM 2025 brief](https://hack.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/hackaTUM2025_logitech_challenge.pdf.pdf)). The finalists of its 2026 DevStudio Challenge included an accessibility plugin (Access Ring) and a voice plugin (VoxRing) ([Logitech blog](https://www.logitech.com/blog/2026/06/02/from-code-to-the-swiss-alps-meet-the-logitech-devstudio-challenge-finalists/)).

Covering several tracks is allowed. LauzHack lets teams work on "one or more" challenges and states that projects are "eligible for multiple prizes" ([rules PDF](https://lauzhack.com/pdf/rules.pdf), [lauzhack.com](https://lauzhack.com)). There are two catches. First, judges punish ideas that look retro-fitted to a contest ([Devpost judging tips](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips)). The safe pattern is one core product in which a single real feature touches each sponsor, not N integrations bolted on. Second, Rule 30 requires you to state explicitly what is new compared with the libraries you reuse ([rules PDF](https://lauzhack.com/pdf/rules.pdf)).

Accessibility also has a well-known trap. "Disability dongles" are student inventions built without disabled input, and sign-language gloves are the textbook case ([Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/322973), [SignAloud open letter](https://faculty.washington.edu/ebender/papers/SignAloudOpenLetter.pdf)). The Swiss Deaf federation SGB-FSS says it knows "**no product that meets our expectations**" ([swissinfo](https://www.swissinfo.ch/ger/schweizer-ki/taugt-k%c3%bcnstliche-intelligenz-f%c3%bcr-geb%c3%a4rdensprache/90131119)). Google now ships free on-device sign-to-text on Pixel 11 ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/blog/putting-sign-language-ai-into-users-hands/)). So **do not build a "sign language translator"**. The concepts below avoid that framing.

## Concept 1 (recommended): Tap gives meetings a silent shoulder-tap

**The problem.** Live captions are now everywhere, but research shows they are not where deaf and hard-of-hearing participants struggle. Auto-captions on major platforms got **about 8% of words wrong** ([Consumer Reports](https://www.consumerreports.org/disability-rights/auto-captions-often-fall-short-on-zoom-facebook-and-others-a9742392879)). In co-design studies, mixed-hearing groups asked for speaker identification and behavioural cues ([McDonnell et al., CHI 2023](https://makeabilitylab.cs.washington.edu/media/publications/McDonnell_EasierOrHarderDependingOnWhoTheHearingPersonIsCodesigningVideoconferencingToolsForSmallGroupsWithMixedHearingStatus_CHI2023.pdf)). A separate CHI study found that captions lose emotion and emphasis ([doi 10.1145/3544548.3580809](https://www.doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580809)). The notes found no product that alerts a deaf or hard-of-hearing user when they are addressed by voice. That is unverified rather than proven absent, so ask a Deaf student or a mentor on Saturday whether Teams or Zoom already does it.

**What it does.** Tap runs in a browser tab next to the meeting. Captions come from the Web Speech API. A tiny rule engine watches the interim transcript for the user's name, question patterns ("what do you think", "any objections", "can you…") and custom keywords. When it fires, the MX Master 4 plays a **`knock`** waveform, the screen edge flashes, and a small card shows the last 10 seconds of transcript plus a one-line LLM summary ("Anna asked you whether the deadline can move to Friday"). On the output side, MediaPipe Face Landmarker blendshapes turn deliberate gestures into actions for users who do not or cannot speak. Raised eyebrows held for 0.8 seconds raise a hand, a nod or head shake answers yes or no, and the button chosen by gaze quadrant is a stretch goal. Each detection is confirmed with a `subtle_collision` tick so the user knows it registered. A daily "meeting-ready" check is the Nexthink feature. Before a call, Tap checks for a frozen camera, a muted or wrong microphone (no speech energy), backlighting and the face out of frame, and gives a one-click fix hint. That makes it something employees open every day even when nothing is broken.

**The wow moment, in the first 30 seconds.** A teammate plays a "colleague" in a fake Zoom call. The demoer wears noise-cancelling headphones playing music. The colleague says "…so Thibaud, are you OK with Friday?" The mouse in the demoer's hand knocks, the card pops up, and the demoer answers by nodding, which posts "Yes" into the chat overlay. Then hand the mouse to the judge and repeat it with the judge's own name.

**Why each track.** For **Logitech**, the webcam and microphone are the senses and the mouse is the actuator. It is the same pattern as Logitech's own Zoom "someone raised a hand" haptic plugin ([Logitech haptics](https://www.logitech.com/en-ch/software/logi-options-plus/haptics.html)), applied to accessibility, and it needs no new gadget. For **IMC**, the pitch is documented exclusion in hybrid work and study, plus the non-speaking use case. For **AXA**, the vision and speech recognition run locally, the LLM sees only short text snippets and only when a trigger fires, and no frames leave the laptop. On a small model the notes estimate a 500-in/100-out call at **about $0.0001**, using third-party pricing for Claude Haiku 5.5 ([techenclave](https://techenclave.com/news/items/claude-haiku-5-5-launches-with-0-10-input-pricing-for-shorter-prompts)), so a whole meeting costs fractions of a cent. Show a live counter of "LLM calls this meeting: 3". For **Nexthink**, the daily readiness check is perceptual: it covers what Spark's endpoint telemetry cannot see ([Nexthink Spark](https://nexthink.com/press/nexthink-spark)). Design deliberately against "bossware" ([CDT](https://cdt.org/?p=12710)): no manager dashboard, no scores, data owned by the user. Say so in the pitch.

**Stack.** TypeScript plus Vite. `@mediapipe/tasks-vision` Face Landmarker gives 478 landmarks and 52 blendshapes; run it in a web worker, because `detect()` blocks the UI thread ([Face Landmarker docs](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/face_landmarker), [Hand Landmarker web guide](https://developers.google.com/edge/mediapipe/solutions/vision/hand_landmarker/web_js?authuser=4)). Captions use the Web Speech API in Chrome or Edge; Edge 139+ supports on-device `processLocally` ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/mdn-api_speechrecognition_processlocally)). The fallback is a Deepgram Nova-3 websocket at **about $0.0048/min** ([Deepgram pricing](https://deepgram.com/pricing)). The LLM is a cheap model behind a 20-line Python or Node proxy. For haptics, go in two stages. First, prototype in hour 1 with the marketplace **HapticWeb** plugin, which exposes a localhost REST/WebSocket API for the MX Master 4 motor ([Logi Marketplace](https://marketplace.logi.com/plugin/HapticWeb/en)). Then build your own C# Actions SDK plugin: declare `HasHapticMapping`, map events to waveforms such as `knock`, `ringing` and `completed`, call `PluginEvents.RaiseEvent()`, and host a localhost WebSocket inside it ([Haptics getting started](https://logitech.github.io/actions-sdk-docs/csharp/haptics/haptics-getting-started)). Your own plugin is the "we built it" answer for Rule 30. Prefer C# over the Node SDK, which is in alpha and Windows-only ([Actions SDK docs](https://logitech.github.io/actions-sdk-docs)). To get the overlay into Zoom or Meet, use an OBS Browser Source and the Virtual Camera ([UT Austin wiki](https://cloud.wikis.utexas.edu/wiki/spaces/comm/pages/33425602/Install+OBS+as+a+virtual+video+switcher+feeding+Zoom)). Let OBS own the physical camera, because Windows may lock it to one app.

**20-hour build plan (A = CV/face, B = Logitech/haptics + backend, C = frontend/captions/UX, D = pitch, user research, testing, video)**

| Window | Goal and milestone |

D talks to the Logitech, IMC and Nexthink booths and looks for a Deaf or hard-of-hearing student or mentor to test with. |

**Fallback cuts, in order.** Drop the gaze quadrant first, then OBS-into-Zoom (show your own mock meeting page instead), then your own C# plugin (keep HapticWeb), then the LLM summary (show the raw 10-second transcript). Never cut the name → knock → nod loop.

**Risks and mitigations.** *Web Speech failing on venue Wi-Fi*: keep the Deepgram fallback and a phone hotspot ready, and keep a pre-recorded audio clip that can be replayed through the pipeline. *HapticWeb routes are undocumented* ([listing](https://marketplace.logi.com/plugin/HapticWeb/en)): test them in hour 1 through its playground, and if they fail go straight to the C# plugin. *Haptics need Options+ 1.95+ and Plugin API 6.2.1+* ([haptics overview](https://logitech.github.io/actions-sdk-docs/csharp/haptics/haptics-overview)): update the mouse firmware on Saturday morning. *False positives from facial expressions*: require a hold time plus a visible "arming" indicator, and calibrate per user. *A judge asks "did you talk to users?"*: test with at least one Deaf or hard-of-hearing person at the event, or say honestly that the next step is testing with SGB-FSS.

## Concept 2: First Signs teaches hearing people LSF-CH, eyebrows included

**The idea.** This flips the burden. Instead of making Deaf people use translation tech, it teaches hearing colleagues, classmates or counter staff the first 20 signs they need, in **LSF-CH, the Swiss French Sign Language used in Lausanne**. The research found no LSF-CH learning tool with camera feedback. SGB-FSS's Signwise gives automatic feedback on about 100 signs, apparently in Swiss German Sign Language (DSGS) ([UZH IICT](https://www.iict.uzh.ch/en/news/Einstufungstest-in-Signwise.html)). The technical edge is **feedback on non-manual markers**. Nvidia's free Signs app launched tracking hands only, with facial expressions and head movement not yet covered ([Nvidia blog](https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/ai-sign-language)). In many sign languages raised eyebrows mark a question, so getting the face wrong changes the meaning. Face Landmarker blendshapes plus Hand Landmarker can check both. The teaching design draws on PopSign AI's finding that **practising sign production teaches more efficiently than receptive-only practice** ([RIT](https://www.rit.edu/ntid/news/parents-deaf-children-can-more-easily-learn-sign-language-thanks-powerful-tech-collaboration)). Structure it as short lessons of "watch → try → get specific correction → use it in a micro-scenario" (meeting a Deaf colleague, a station counter). The LLM only turns landmark diffs into one-sentence coaching such as "your hand is right, but lift your eyebrows: this is a question". It is not a chatbot.

**The wow moment.** A judge who has never signed learns "Do you need help?" in 90 seconds. The mouse gives a `subtle_collision` tick when the handshape locks in and fires a `completed` or `firework` waveform when hands and face are both right. A "ghost hand" overlay shows exactly where the judge's hand drifted.

**Tracks.** *Scholé* is the strongest fit of any concept: a memorable experience that measurably teaches a novice, and not a chatbot. *IMC*: community inclusion. *AXA*: landmark-only, kNN or dynamic time warping (DTW) in the browser, no video uploaded; this mirrors how Google's own system extracts landmarks on-device ([DeepMind](https://deepmind.google/blog/putting-sign-language-ai-into-users-hands/)). *Logitech*: webcam plus haptic tutor, though "productivity workflow" is a stretch. *SBB*: optional "20 signs for station staff" lesson pack.

**Build plan.** Hours 0-2: pick the 15-20 signs from a reputable LSF-CH reference, and ideally have a Deaf signer at the event record or validate them. Hours 2-8: A records 10-30 samples per sign and builds a kNN classifier for static signs on normalized 21×3 hand landmarks and DTW for moving ones, which the notes estimate at **6-10 hours for a polished demo**; B builds the blendshape checks for eyebrows and head; C builds the lesson UI and B also wires up HapticWeb. Hours 8-14: scoring, coaching text, progress and spaced repetition, a before/after quiz to prove that it teaches. Hours 14-20: polish, freeze, rehearse. **Cuts:** keep static handshapes and the eyebrow check, and drop moving signs. Scope 10 signs, not 20.

**Risks.** *Accuracy is good for the person who recorded the samples and noticeably worse for others* (technical notes, inferred), so record samples from all four team members and calibrate per user. *Teaching wrong LSF-CH* is the critical risk: without a Deaf validator you could ship errors, so show your source on screen and frame it as a "prototype, to be co-designed with SGB-FSS". *Community backlash*: Deaf communities are openly sceptical of "help" tech ([arXiv 2602.10235](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2602.10235)). Hearing-people-learn framing is the defensible version, and survey respondents want tools to be Deaf-led ([Northeastern](https://news.northeastern.edu/2026/03/19/sign-language-technology-skepticism/)).

## Concept 3: Intent Ring maps face gestures to whole workflows

**The idea.** Head-mouse cursor control is already free and solved: Google Project Gameface is open source ([Google Developers Blog](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/project-gameface-launches-on-android/)), and Apple ships Head Pointer and Eye Tracking ([MacRumors](https://www.macrumors.com/2024/05/15/ios-18-accessibility-features)). So skip the cursor and build the **intent layer** above it. A few reliable face gestures plus short voice commands trigger high-level actions such as "join my next meeting", "mute", "next slide" or "read me the last message", through an Actions SDK plugin and the on-screen Actions Ring. The Ring can be activated by any MX device and then used with any mouse ([supported devices](https://logitech.github.io/actions-sdk-docs/supported-devices)). An optional differentiator, which is a hypothesis rather than a verified gap: *fatigue-aware adaptation*. When blendshape noise or misfires rise, the system enlarges targets or switches to voice.

**Wow moment.** A teammate with hands taped to the desk joins a call, shares slides and answers a message using only eyebrows, a smile-click and two spoken words, with a haptic confirmation for each step.

**Tracks.** Logitech is strong (Actions Ring plus haptics plus webcam, and Access Ring was a 2026 DevStudio finalist ([Logitech blog](https://www.logitech.com/blog/2026/06/02/from-code-to-the-swiss-alps-meet-the-logitech-devstudio-challenge-finalists/))). IMC is medium, AXA medium and Nexthink medium. **Why it ranks third:** it is the most crowded theme, and judges who know Gameface will ask what is new. Do not rely on webcam gaze. WebGazer-class tracking is accurate only to **about 4° of visual angle (about 170-210 px)** ([WebGazer paper](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10024076)), which is good enough for a 2×2 grid but not for a cursor. Use head pose plus blendshapes, which the notes estimate at about 2-4 hours. **Plan:** gesture engine in hours 0-6, C# plugin with 6 actions in hours 6-12, voice and fatigue mode in hours 12-16, then freeze. **Cuts:** drop fatigue adaptation first, then voice.

## Concept 4: Platform Ears matches announcements to your own journey

**The idea.** This is a disruption companion for deaf travellers. The laptop or phone microphone transcribes a station or train announcement, matches it against the user's saved journey and live SIRI-SX event data, and pushes an easy-language reroute card plus a haptic or vibration alert. The SIRI-SX endpoint is `api.opentransportdata.swiss/la/siri-sx` and needs an API key ([opendata.swiss](https://opendata.swiss/en/dataset/ereignisinformation-ov-schweiz-siri-sx-vdv736)); GTFS-RT Service Alerts are an alternative ([GTFS-RT SA](https://ckan.opendata.swiss/dataset/gtfs-realtime-service-alerts1)). SBB Inclusive already pushes disruption text to blind and partially sighted users, with deaf users as secondary beneficiaries ([SBB Inclusive](https://www.sbb.ch/en/travel-information/apps/sbb-inclusive.html)). The open gap is *audio announcements matched to your own trip*, along with short pre-recorded LSF-CH clips made with real signers instead of generated avatars.

**Tracks and verdict.** SBB is strong, IMC and AXA medium, and Logitech weak, because a webcam adds little and a mouse is not with you on a platform. Pick this concept only if SBB's prize matters more to the team than Logitech's. **Plan:** request the API key at 12:00 Saturday and cache a recorded alert sample as the demo fallback. Build ASR plus fuzzy station and train matching in hours 0-8 and the LLM easy-language card in hours 8-14. **Cuts:** drop live data and replay recorded alerts. **Risk:** the notes could not confirm that loudspeaker announcements exist as a machine-readable stream, so the microphone is the only route.

## Engineering rules that apply to every concept

Run **one** heavy in-browser model at most. One MediaPipe landmarker (or Holistic), plus Web Speech, plus a cloud LLM called on triggers is safe on a laptop. Adding in-browser Whisper and WebLLM risks dropped frames and VRAM exhaustion, and one user saw VRAM hit nearly 100% within about two minutes in a realtime-Whisper demo ([HF discussion](https://huggingface.co/spaces/Xenova/realtime-whisper-webgpu/discussions/1)). Serve the `.task` and wasm files locally. Camera and microphone require HTTPS or localhost. Keep one stable demo URL, because permissions are stored per origin. Turn perception into compact text events ("NAME_MENTIONED", "BROW_RAISE 0.82") before anything reaches an LLM: it is your AXA story and your latency story at once. Show a visible detection overlay so judges can see the system working ([Devpost judging tips](https://info.devpost.com/blog/hackathon-judging-tips)). Judging runs Sun 12:30-15:00 across roughly 70 projects ([lauzhack.com](https://lauzhack.com), [2025 projects](https://2025.lauzhack.com/projects)), so prepare a 30-second, a 2-minute and a per-sponsor 60-second version, and have a working moment on screen within 90 seconds ([JetBrains blog](https://blog.jetbrains.com/ai/2026/06/how-to-win-a-hackathon-notes-from-the-judging-table/)).

## Conclusion

**Build Tap.** It is the only concept where the challenge's core idea, the webcam and microphone as the AI's senses with Logitech hardware as its output, is also the accessibility solution, the frugal-AI argument and the reason to open it every day. The demo works on any judge in ten seconds: say their name and the mouse in their hand knocks. Reach for Scholé only by choosing First Signs *instead*, not by bolting a lesson onto Tap. A sign-learning tab inside a meeting tool is exactly the forced fit that judges punish. If the team goes for First Signs, the deciding factor is whether you can find a Deaf LSF-CH signer by Saturday 14:00 to check the signs. Without one, the risk of teaching errors outweighs the Scholé prize.

One open point could change the plan, so check it on Saturday morning. The research could not confirm that no meeting platform already offers voice-triggered "you were addressed" alerts. Ask the Logitech booth and a Deaf or hard-of-hearing attendee. If something like it exists, keep the same build and move the novelty to the non-speaking face-gesture replies and the haptic channel, which nothing in the notes duplicates.
